How we invest

Our portfolios are designed to be boring for a reason. The interesting part of investing is the part that costs money.

Read the case for boring
  • Cost is the only input we control with certainty.Every basis point of fund expense, trading cost and avoidable tax compounds against you for as long as you own the portfolio. We treat cost as a first-order decision.
  • Diversify globally. Concentrate deliberately.Core portfolios hold thousands of securities across every developed and emerging market. Concentration is allowed only when you have chosen it, sized it and understand what it can cost.
  • Rebalance by rule, not by mood.Each portfolio has written target ranges. When markets push an allocation outside its range, we trade it back. Headlines do not enter the process.
  • Every trade is a tax decision.Asset location, lot selection, loss harvesting and gain deferral are built into the way we manage taxable accounts, because after-tax return is the only return you get to keep.

How the firm came to be

Marcus Whitfield and Elena Castellanos founded Ashlar Capital Partners in April 2009, in a borrowed conference room on Madison Avenue, six months after the collapse of Lehman Brothers. Marcus had run a private wealth team at a global investment bank. Elena had managed a multi-asset portfolio for an asset manager. Both had watched clients pay for advice that was shaped, quietly and constantly, by what the institution needed to sell that quarter.

The firm opened with eleven client families and $140 million. It grew the way we hoped it would: slowly, by referral, one family at a time. Today Ashlar manages $4.2 billion for roughly 1,800 households and a small number of endowments, foundations and family offices, with 64 people across offices in New York, Greenwich, Boston and Palm Beach.

Some things have not changed since 2009. The firm is 100% owned by the people who work here, with no outside investors and no plans to sell. We have never manufactured a product, accepted a referral payment or earned a commission. Every client is served by a named team of at least three people, and the average tenure of that team is nine years. When an advisor retires, the transition is planned two years in advance and the client meets their next advisor long before they need one.

About the name

Ashlar is finely dressed stone, cut square and laid in even courses. It is the masonry you see in the buildings that have stood for centuries, and it is slow, deliberate work: each block is measured against the last, and nothing is set that will not carry weight. We chose the name because it describes what we think good financial advice is. Not clever, not fast, but square, well fitted and built to be added to.

The case for boring: why our core portfolios rarely change

By Elena Castellanos, CFA, CFP®August 27, 20266 min read

In a typical year, a client's core portfolio at Ashlar will contain somewhere between eight and fifteen trades. Most of them are rebalancing trades, selling a little of what has grown and buying a little of what has lagged. Some are loss harvests in taxable accounts. A few move cash in or out to fund a tax payment, a tuition bill or a house. What the portfolio will not contain is a move out of stocks because someone on television was worried, or into a sector because it had a good quarter.

New clients sometimes find this unsettling. They have come from firms where the quarterly letter described a series of decisive actions, and it is easy to confuse activity with care. So it is worth explaining, plainly, why our portfolios are designed to be boring and why we think the boredom is where most of the value lives.

Three reasons we do not make market calls

The first is evidence. Decades of academic research and the published records of professional managers agree on one thing: almost no one moves reliably between asset classes at the right time, and the few who appear to are difficult to distinguish from luck in advance. We have no reason to believe we are the exception, and we would rather not charge clients for the experiment.

The second is cost. Every tactical trade has a spread, a commission and, in a taxable account, a tax bill. A portfolio that turns over 50% a year can easily give up a full percentage point annually to taxes alone. Over twenty-five years, that is the difference between a portfolio that quadruples and one that triples. The market does not owe you that percentage point back.

The third is behavior, ours and yours. A written process that says when to trade and when not to is the only reliable defense against the instinct to do something when markets fall. We are not immune to that instinct. We have simply built a firm in which it is not allowed to make decisions.

What actually changes

Every portfolio has a written target for each asset class and a range around it. We use relative bands of 20%: a 42% U.S. equity target is rebalanced when the actual weight climbs above roughly 50% or falls below roughly 34%. Markets push allocations outside their bands a few times a year, and when they do, we trade back to target, using new cash first and sales second so that the tax cost is as small as possible.

In taxable accounts, the direct indexing sleeve is harvesting losses continuously. A position that has fallen below cost is sold and replaced with a similar but not identical security, the loss is banked against current or future gains, and the portfolio's exposure barely moves. In a year like 2022, that process generated losses equal to several years of realized gains for many clients. In a strong year it generates less, which is the point.

The strategic allocation itself, the 65/35 split or the weight given to international stocks, is reviewed by the Investment Committee once a year. It has changed twice since 2009: once to add inflation-protected bonds as a permanent holding and once to reduce the home-country bias in the equity sleeve. Both changes were argued about for months and implemented over a full year.

What never changes

Your allocation to stocks does not change because of a forecast. It changes when your plan changes: a retirement date moves, a business is sold, a child's education is funded. Those are the events that alter how much risk a household should carry, and they are the only ones that alter how much risk we take on its behalf.

The interesting part of investing is the part that costs money. We try to leave it to other people.

Boring, in other words, is a feature. It is cheaper, it is more tax-efficient, and it is far easier to stick with for the thirty years it takes compounding to do its work. If you want a portfolio that is a source of news, there are many firms that will build one. If you want one that you can forget about for long stretches while it quietly does what it was designed to do, that is the one we build.

Roth conversions in a low-income year: a practical guide

By Priya Raman, CPA, CFP®August 14, 20268 min read

A Roth conversion is a simple transaction with complicated consequences. You move money from a pre-tax retirement account, such as a traditional IRA or a rolled-over 401(k), into a Roth IRA. The amount converted is added to your taxable income for the year. In exchange, every dollar of future growth comes out tax-free, there are no required minimum distributions during your lifetime, and your heirs inherit an account that will not be taxed on withdrawal.

Whether that trade is a good one depends almost entirely on one comparison: the tax rate you pay on the conversion today against the rate you, or your heirs, would otherwise pay on the same dollars later. That is why the best conversions happen in years when your income is unusually low.

Where low-income years come from

They are more common than people expect. The most valuable window is the stretch between retirement and the start of Social Security and required minimum distributions, which for most of our clients runs from the early sixties to age 73, or 75 for those born in 1960 or later. During those years a household can have a large balance sheet and a very small taxable income. Other windows include a sabbatical, a year between jobs, a year in which a business shows a loss, and a year with a large deduction such as a major charitable gift.

How to size a conversion

The method is to fill a bracket. For 2026, married couples filing jointly pay 22% on taxable income up to about $211,000 and 24% up to about $403,000; single filers reach those points at roughly half the income. A couple with $80,000 of taxable income who expect to be in the 24% bracket or higher in retirement can convert about $130,000 at 22% and a further $190,000 at 24% before crossing into the 32% bracket. Whether to stop at the top of the 22% bracket or the 24% bracket depends on what you expect later, and we model both.

Remember that taxable income is what matters, after the standard or itemized deduction. A couple with $32,200 of standard deduction in 2026 and $60,000 of other income has $27,800 of taxable income, and room in the low brackets that is easy to overlook.

The thresholds that bite

Federal brackets are not the only line on the map. Several others depend on modified adjusted gross income and can turn a well-intentioned conversion into an expensive one.

  • Medicare premiums. If you are 63 or older, this year's income sets your Medicare Part B and Part D surcharges two years from now. In 2026 the first surcharge tier begins at about $218,000 of MAGI for joint filers and $109,000 for single filers. Crossing a tier by one dollar costs a couple more than $1,900 for the year.
  • Health insurance subsidies. If you are under 65 and buying coverage on an exchange, conversion income reduces or eliminates premium tax credits.
  • Net investment income tax. The conversion itself is not investment income, but it raises MAGI and can push interest, dividends and gains above the $250,000 threshold where the 3.8% tax applies.
  • State income tax. New York and Connecticut tax conversions as ordinary income. Florida does not. If a move is already planned, the conversion may be worth deferring until after it.
  • Social Security taxation and the senior deduction. Conversion income can increase the taxable share of benefits and phases out the additional deduction available to taxpayers 65 and older.

Rules that are easy to get wrong

Conversions are irrevocable. Since 2018 there has been no way to undo one, so we convert in tranches through the year and finish in December, when the year's income is known. If you are already subject to required minimum distributions, the RMD must be taken first and cannot itself be converted. Each conversion starts its own five-year clock before the converted principal can be withdrawn without penalty if you are under 59½, and the Roth account itself must be five years old before earnings are tax-free. And the tax should be paid from a taxable account, not from the conversion; withholding tax from the converted amount shrinks the Roth and, under 59½, can trigger a penalty on the withheld portion.

Why heirs matter more than they used to

Most non-spouse beneficiaries must now empty an inherited IRA within ten years. For a child in peak earning years, that means a large pre-tax IRA is drained at 35% or 37%, often at exactly the wrong time. A Roth passes to the same child with no income tax at all. When a client's plan shows the IRA will mostly go to the next generation, the case for converting at 22% or 24% today becomes very strong.

How we do it

Each fall we project the year's return for every client and identify the conversion room. We convert in-kind where possible, moving depressed positions so that the recovery happens inside the Roth, and we set aside cash for the estimated payment so there is no surprise in April. The conversion is reviewed with your CPA before it happens, not after. Done this way, a series of modest conversions over a decade of low-income years is among the highest-value planning moves available to a retiring household, and among the least dramatic.

Tax and planning

Concentrated stock: four ways to diversify without a tax bomb

By Priya Raman, CPA, CFP®July 30, 20267 min read

A surprising number of the families we meet have most of their net worth in one stock. Sometimes it is the company they built or the company they work for. Sometimes it is an inheritance, or a position bought decades ago that has simply grown. Whatever the source, the problem is the same: the position is too large to be safe and too appreciated to sell without a large tax bill, so nothing happens, year after year.

We consider a position concentrated when it exceeds 10% to 15% of a household's investable assets. Above that level, the outcome of the household's plan depends on the fortunes of a single company, and the history of single companies, even excellent ones, is not reassuring. Here are the four approaches we use, in the order we usually consider them.

1. Staged selling, paired with loss harvesting

The simplest strategy is still the best one for most people: sell a fixed amount on a fixed schedule, quarterly or monthly, over two to five years. The discipline removes the temptation to wait for a better price, and spreading sales across tax years keeps more of the gain in the 15% federal capital gains bracket and below the surcharge thresholds. For executives and insiders, a 10b5-1 plan puts the schedule in writing in advance and provides an affirmative defense against insider trading claims.

The strategy becomes materially more powerful when the proceeds go into a direct indexed portfolio. That portfolio harvests losses continuously, and those losses offset the gains from the next tranche of sales. Over a multi-year program, clients frequently shelter a third or more of the total gain this way, with no exotic structures involved.

2. Exchange funds

An exchange fund pools concentrated positions from many investors into a single partnership. You contribute your shares and receive units representing a diversified basket. Under Section 721 there is no gain on the contribution. After seven years you can redeem units for a diversified portfolio of securities, still without triggering the original gain, which carries over to the new holdings.

The costs are real. The fund must hold at least 20% of its assets in qualifying illiquid property, usually real estate, and management fees are typically 0.75% to 1.5% a year. The seven-year lock-up is firm, and the basket is chosen by the fund, not by you. Exchange funds work best for very large positions held by investors who have no need for the money and no strong view on what they would rather own.

3. Giving it away, strategically

If charitable giving is already part of your plan, appreciated stock is the best asset to give. Donating shares held more than one year to a donor-advised fund produces a deduction for full market value, up to 30% of adjusted gross income, and the gain is never taxed. Many clients fund several years of giving at once in a high-income year, then grant from the fund over time.

A charitable remainder trust goes further. The trust sells the stock without paying tax, reinvests the full proceeds in a diversified portfolio, and pays you an income stream for life or a term of years, with the remainder going to charity. The trade-off is that a meaningful share of the value, at least 10% by actuarial calculation and usually more, leaves the family. For clients who are charitably inclined and want income, it can be the most tax-efficient structure available.

4. Hedging without selling

A collar buys a put option below the current price and sells a call above it, often at no net cost, limiting both the downside and the upside for a period of one to three years. A prepaid variable forward adds a loan against the hedged position, providing liquidity today with the sale deferred. Both are useful when a sale is restricted or when a step-up in basis at death is a realistic part of the plan.

Both also require care. If the collar is too tight, the IRS treats the position as constructively sold under Section 1259 and taxes the gain immediately. Hedging costs money, the counterparty is usually a bank, and the documentation is complex. We use these tools for a small number of clients with very large positions, and never as a substitute for a plan to diversify.

Two more considerations

For founders and early employees, qualified small business stock treatment can exclude a large portion of the gain, and the exclusion can be multiplied by gifting shares to trusts for family members before a sale. For older holders, the step-up in cost basis at death can make holding a portion of the position through the estate the most tax-efficient choice of all, provided the risk is acceptable in the meantime.

What we recommend most often is the first strategy, because it is transparent, reversible and easy to live with. A tax bill that is spread over five years and cut by a third through harvesting is a bill most families are happy to pay for the privilege of no longer lying awake over one ticker symbol.

What a fee-only fiduciary actually means, and the conflicts we still have

By Marcus J. Whitfield, CFAJune 18, 20265 min read

Two phrases appear on the website of nearly every wealth management firm: fiduciary and fee-only. They are used loosely enough that they have started to lose meaning. Since we use both, it seems fair to explain exactly what they mean, how you can check whether they are true, and where they fall short of describing a firm with no conflicts at all, because no such firm exists.

Fiduciary

An investment adviser registered under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 owes its clients a fiduciary duty: a duty of care and a duty of loyalty, at all times, on every piece of advice. That is a legal standard enforced by the Securities and Exchange Commission, and it is the standard Ashlar operates under.

Brokerage firms operate under a different rule, Regulation Best Interest, which requires that a recommendation be in the client's best interest at the moment it is made but does not impose an ongoing duty and permits the firm to be paid by the products it recommends. Many large firms are registered as both, and their advisors can switch between the two standards depending on the account. When a firm says it is a fiduciary, the follow-up question is: always, and on every account?

Fee-only

Fee-only means the firm is paid only by its clients. Not by fund companies, not by insurers, not by banks for referring loans, not by anyone whose product ends up in your portfolio. The term is often confused with fee-based, which means the firm charges fees and also earns commissions. The distinction is the whole point.

How to check in ten minutes

Every registered adviser files a Form ADV with the SEC, available to anyone through the Investment Adviser Public Disclosure website. Part 2A is the brochure. Item 5 describes how the firm is paid; if the words commission, 12b-1 or revenue sharing appear, the firm is not fee-only. Item 10 lists affiliations with broker-dealers, banks and insurers. Item 12 describes brokerage practices and whether the firm receives anything from the custodian it recommends. The shorter Form CRS summarizes the same information in two pages and is required to be given to every new client. If a firm's website and its ADV disagree, believe the ADV.

The conflicts we still have

A fee calculated on assets under management removes most conflicts, but it creates a few of its own, and we would rather name them than pretend otherwise.

  • We are paid more when you keep assets invested with us. That gives us an incentive to recommend rolling a 401(k) into an IRA we manage, to discourage paying off a mortgage or buying an annuity, and to suggest that gifts to children be made from accounts we do not manage. We address this by documenting, in writing, every recommendation that reduces the assets we bill on, and by reviewing those recommendations in our compliance program each year.
  • We are paid on the same schedule whether markets rise or fall, which means our revenue moves with the market rather than with the quality of our work. We think this is the least bad way to be paid, but it is not a perfect one.
  • We recommend custodians whose platforms we know well. We receive no payment from them, but we do receive research, technology and support that make our business easier to run.

The full list is in our Form ADV Part 2A and in our Form CRS, both of which we would rather you read than take our word for. A firm that cannot describe its own conflicts has either not looked for them or would prefer you did not.

Estate planning around a liquidity event

By Priya Raman, CPA, CFP®May 21, 20267 min read

The most valuable estate planning of a business owner's life happens in a window that closes the day the sale is signed. Before that day, the company can be valued conservatively, gifted in pieces and moved into structures that take future growth out of the taxable estate. After it, there is only cash, and cash is worth exactly what it says on the wire confirmation.

We begin this work eighteen months to two years before an expected transaction. Here is what it involves, and what remains to be done once the money has arrived.

Start with the exemption

For 2026 the federal estate and gift tax exemption is $15 million per person, $30 million for a married couple, and it is now indexed for inflation without a scheduled sunset. Above that amount, the federal tax is 40%. The annual exclusion allows an additional $19,000 per recipient each year, free of gift tax and without using any exemption.

Three states matter for most of our clients. New York exempts just over $7 million, but with a cliff: an estate more than 5% above the exemption is taxed from the first dollar, at rates up to 16%. Connecticut's exemption matches the federal amount. Massachusetts exempts $2 million, with no cliff since 2023. Florida has no estate tax, which is why the question of residency comes up in nearly every pre-sale conversation, and why the answer has to be genuine: a change of domicile that is documented in facts, not just on paper.

Before the sale

The core strategy is to give away shares while their value is low and let the growth happen outside your estate. The instruments differ in what they trade away.

  • Spousal lifetime access trusts. Each spouse funds a trust for the benefit of the other, using exemption, so the assets are out of both estates while a spouse retains indirect access. Careful drafting is required to avoid the trusts being treated as reciprocal.
  • Grantor retained annuity trusts. You transfer shares to a trust that pays you back an annuity over two to five years; any appreciation above the IRS hurdle rate passes to the beneficiaries with little or no gift tax. A GRAT funded a year before a sale at a premium can be extraordinarily efficient.
  • Donor-advised funds and charitable lead trusts. Gifting shares to a donor-advised fund before a sale produces a full-value deduction and removes the gain entirely. A charitable lead trust pays charity first and passes the remainder to family at a discounted gift value.
  • Qualified small business stock. If the company qualifies under Section 1202, each shareholder can exclude up to $10 million of gain, or $15 million for stock issued after July 4, 2025, with partial exclusions available after three and four years for that newer stock. Gifting shares to non-grantor trusts for family members before the sale can multiply the exclusion, and the analysis should be completed before any letter of intent is signed.

Every one of these requires a qualified appraisal and time. Gifts made in the weeks before a signed deal invite the IRS to argue that the true value was the sale price. This is the main reason we insist on starting early.

After the sale

The first instruction is to do very little for a while. Proceeds go into Treasury bills and a short municipal ladder while the plan is finalized, and the estimated tax on the sale is set aside on day one. Most clients underestimate the emotional weight of a liquidity event, and decisions made in the first ninety days are rarely improved by speed.

Then the work resumes. Trusts funded before the sale now hold cash and need an investment policy. The remaining estate needs a plan for the exemption that was not used. Beneficiary designations, titling and insurance need to be redone for a balance sheet that looks nothing like the old one. And the family needs a conversation, often the first, about what the money is for. We have found that clients who have that conversation within the first year make better decisions for decades afterward.

How we think about private markets in a family portfolio

By Elena Castellanos, CFA, CFP®April 23, 20266 min read

Few topics come up more often in client meetings than private markets. The large university endowments have earned strong long-run returns with heavy allocations to private equity, venture and real assets, and a growing number of products now offer the same exposure to individual investors with far lower minimums. The question we hear is some version of: should we have that too?

Our answer is sometimes, in measured size, and only after being honest about three things the marketing tends to leave out.

The endowment model does not transfer cleanly

An endowment is permanent. It has no mortality, no divorce, no children buying houses and no business that might need rescuing. It can commit to a fund that locks up capital for twelve years and spend from a portfolio that is a third illiquid, because its liabilities are a steady 5% a year forever. A family's liabilities are lumpy and unpredictable, and the cost of being forced to sell an illiquid interest at a discount, or to skip a capital call, is far higher than the cost of holding a public portfolio through a bad year.

Dispersion is the whole story

The average private equity fund, after fees, has historically delivered returns not far from what a public small-cap portfolio delivered over the same periods. The best funds have done dramatically better, and the worst dramatically worse, and the gap between the top and bottom quartile is many times wider than in public equity. That means private markets are not an asset class you can buy; they are a manager selection problem. Access to the top quartile is limited, persistence is weaker than it once was, and the fee structure, typically 1.5% to 2% a year plus 20% of profits, is a tax on every dollar of skill you fail to find.

Reported volatility is not real volatility

Private assets are valued quarterly by their managers, and those valuations move slowly. A portfolio that shows a 4% decline in a year when public equities fell 20% has not avoided risk; it has avoided measuring it. Some investors value that smoothing for its own sake, and there is a behavioral argument for it. But a plan that relies on private marks to look calm is not calmer, and we would rather clients understood what they own.

Where we use them

With those caveats stated, we do build private allocations for clients who qualify as qualified purchasers, generally $5 million or more in investments, and whose plans show they will not need the committed capital for at least ten years. Our practice is:

  • Total private exposure between 5% and 20% of the portfolio, sized to the plan's liquidity rather than to an aspirational target.
  • Commitments paced over three or four vintage years, so that no single year's pricing dominates and the portfolio is self-funding once distributions begin.
  • A preference for private credit and secondaries early in the program, which return cash sooner, with buyout and real assets added as distributions arrive.
  • Evergreen and interval funds used selectively, with the understanding that quarterly redemption limits, typically 5%, are a feature until the day everyone wants out at once.
  • No placement fees, no revenue sharing and no proprietary funds. We are paid the same whether a client holds private investments or not.

For clients who prefer to skip all of this, we say so plainly: a low-cost, globally diversified public portfolio has been an excellent way to compound wealth for a century, and no household needs private markets to have a successful plan. The clients who add them should do so because they understand the trade, not because a neighbor's portfolio looked calmer last year.

Teaching the next generation: a family governance primer

By Rebecca Hartley, CPWA®March 12, 20265 min read

Most estate plans we review are technically sound and practically unprepared. The trusts are drafted, the exemptions are used, the beneficiary forms are correct. The people who will receive the money have never been told it exists, what it is for or what will be expected of them. That is the gap that turns a well-planned estate into a family argument, and it is the one that documents cannot close.

Family governance is the unglamorous name for closing it. It means agreeing, out loud and in advance, on what the family's wealth is for and how decisions about it will be made. Here is the order of operations we use with client families, adjusted for the ages of the people involved.

Start with values, not numbers

The first conversation is not about how much. It is about what the money should do: education, opportunity, security, philanthropy, a business, a place. Many families find it useful to write down a short statement of purpose, a paragraph rather than a mission document, and to revisit it every few years. Everything that follows, from trust distribution standards to the choice of trustee, is easier when there is a sentence to test it against.

Disclose in stages

  • Twelve to eighteen. How money works: earning, saving, compounding, taxes, the difference between price and value. An allowance with real decisions attached teaches more than any lecture. This is not the time for numbers about the family's wealth.
  • The twenties. The structure: that trusts exist, roughly what they are for, who the trustees are and what the distribution standards say. Young adults handle this better than parents expect, provided the information comes with expectations attached.
  • The thirties and beyond. The numbers, the plan and a seat at the table. By this point an heir should have met the family's advisors, understood the investment policy and, ideally, made some decisions with real money.

Use philanthropy as the training ground

A donor-advised fund is the best classroom we know. Giving children a share of the annual grant budget to allocate, with a requirement to research and present their choices, teaches due diligence, disagreement and follow-through with stakes that matter and consequences that are survivable. Many of the families we work with hold a short grants meeting each year that doubles as the family's only regular financial conversation.

Hold a meeting, and keep holding it

An annual family meeting, an hour or two with an agenda, is where governance actually happens. The agenda is usually short: a review of the purpose statement, an update on the plan from the advisors, any decisions that need the family's input, and the grants discussion. The first meeting is awkward. The fifth is routine, and by then the family has a habit that will outlast its founders.

What to avoid

Surprise inheritances. Secrecy about the existence of trusts. Choosing a trustee because of birth order rather than temperament. Skipping the prenuptial conversation because it feels unkind, when a clear agreement protects the marriage from the family's wealth as much as the reverse. And waiting for the right moment, which never comes on its own. The best time to start was a decade ago. The second best time is the next family dinner.

Investing

Direct indexing, explained

By Naomi Feldman, CFAFebruary 19, 20265 min read

Since 2023, the default for a taxable equity account at Ashlar above $250,000 has not been an index fund. It has been the index itself: a few hundred individual stocks, weighted to track a broad U.S. or global benchmark, held in the client's own account. Clients reasonably ask why we would do something more complicated than buying a fund that costs three basis points. The answer is taxes.

The mechanics

An index fund is a single security. Inside it, individual stocks rise and fall, but from your perspective there is one position with one cost basis, and losses inside the fund are of no use to you. When you own the stocks directly, every position has its own basis, and in any given month some of them are below it. Those positions are sold, the loss is realized and the proceeds are immediately reinvested in a similar company or a basket that keeps the portfolio's exposure intact. The wash sale rule prevents buying the same stock back within thirty days, so the replacement must be different, but it does not need to be very different.

The realized losses offset capital gains anywhere in your return: the sale of a business, a concentrated stock, a rental property or a fund with a large distribution. Up to $3,000 a year can offset ordinary income, and unused losses carry forward indefinitely. For a client with gains to shelter, the after-tax benefit in the early years is commonly between half a percent and one and a half percent of the account's value annually, with the higher figures in volatile markets.

Customization

Owning the stocks also allows the portfolio to be shaped around you. An executive can exclude her own company and its closest competitors, so that the portfolio does not double up on a risk she already carries. A family can exclude industries it prefers not to own. And a client transferring in a legacy portfolio of appreciated stocks can keep those positions inside the index sleeve, selling them gradually as harvested losses become available, rather than liquidating on day one.

The honest limits

Direct indexing is not free. The manager fee is higher than an ETF's, and there is tracking error, usually a fraction of a percent a year, because the portfolio is not the index exactly. More importantly, the benefit declines. In a rising market, positions that were harvested are replaced at higher cost, and after several years most of the portfolio sits well above its basis with little left to harvest. Practitioners call this ossification. The strategy front-loads its value, which is fine if you have gains to use it against and less compelling if you do not.

That is why we do not apply it everywhere. It belongs in taxable accounts, above a size where the trading costs are trivial, for clients who have or will have capital gains to offset. In retirement accounts, or for a client whose only gains are the ones inside the portfolio, a plain index fund does the job at a lower cost, and we use it.
